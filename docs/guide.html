Setup

Getting started

Driftwood needs macOS 14 or later, and runs on Apple silicon and Intel. It asks for no system permission at all — see what it asks for on the front page.

Install

Homebrew

brew trust --cask gapmiss/tap/driftwood   # required for third-party taps
brew install --cask gapmiss/tap/driftwood

The cask installs the same ad-hoc-signed app the DMG carries, so the first-launch step below applies either way.

The DMG

Download Driftwood.dmg from the latest release, open it, and drag Driftwood to Applications. Then read the next heading before you double-click it.

From source

You need a Swift toolchain — Xcode or the Command Line Tools. There is no Xcode project; the build is SPM behind a Makefile.

git clone https://github.com/gapmiss/driftwood
cd driftwood
make run

make run builds the app, assembles .build/Driftwood.app with its Info.plist and an ad-hoc signature, and opens it. A build from source is not quarantined, so none of the Gatekeeper steps below apply to it.

The first launch is blocked, and that is expected

Driftwood is signed ad-hoc rather than notarized, so macOS refuses to open it the first time with a message about it being damaged or from an unidentified developer. Nothing is damaged. The app has a valid signature; it has not been through Apple's notary service. Unblocking it is a one-time step, and which step depends on your macOS version.

Your macOSWhat to do, once
15 (Sequoia) and later Double-click Driftwood and let it be blocked. Open System Settings → Privacy & Security, scroll to Security, and click Open Anyway next to the message about Driftwood. Confirm with your password or Touch ID.
14 (Sonoma) Right-click Driftwood in Applications, choose Open, then Open again in the dialog.
Either, from a terminal xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine /Applications/Driftwood.app removes the quarantine flag, after which the app opens normally.

Where the app is once it is running

Nowhere you can see. Driftwood has no Dock icon, no menu bar item and no window until you ask for one. Press ⌃⌥T and the panel appears with the keyboard already in it. Press it again, while you are still typing in it, and the panel goes away.

The panel and its right-click menu are the whole interface. Right-click anywhere in the panel for themes, opacity, tabs and Quit — there is no preferences window to look for.

If the panel will not appear at all and you want the app gone, quit it from a terminal:

pkill -x Driftwood

That is also the answer if a hotkey conflict has left you with no way to summon the panel; see Troubleshooting.

Core

The panel

Summon and dismiss

⌃⌥T always leaves you with a panel you can type in, and only puts it away when the panel is already what you were typing in. So it shows the panel when it is hidden, hands it the keyboard when it is on screen but you have been working somewhere else, and hides it when you were just typing in it.

⌃⌥F is the same thing without the last step: it never hides the panel. Bind it if you would rather the summon key could not put the panel away by accident.

The app behind never loses focus

Summon the panel and it takes your keystrokes, but the app you were working in stays the frontmost app. Its title bar stays lit and its text cursor stays where you left it. Dismiss the panel and you are back where you were, with nothing to click on first.

Driftwood asks for no Accessibility permission to do this, and macOS never prompts you for one.

Moving and resizing

ToDo
Move the panel Drag the empty part of the tab strip — there is always some, to the left of the “+” — or hold ⌘ and drag anywhere in the panel.
Resize Drag any edge or corner. The grab target is the outer 6 points, which is why a drag that starts on text selects text instead.
Put it back Reset Position in the right-click menu returns it to 720×360, centred on the main display.

At the corners the cursor shows a horizontal resize arrow rather than a diagonal one. Drag it anyway — a corner drag resizes in both directions.

The panel will not shrink below 20 columns by 2 rows, so its smallest size moves with the font size.

It comes back where you left it

Driftwood saves the panel's size and position shortly after you stop moving or resizing it, and puts the panel back there at the next launch. The saved position is checked against the displays you have at that moment, which may not be the ones you had:

  • Saved on a display that no longer exists → reset to the default size and position.
  • Hanging off the edge of a display that does exist → slid back into view, at the same size. It is never resized to fit.
  • Less than 80 points of it left showing → reset, rather than left somewhere you would have to hunt for it.

Where the panel sits

Above other windows, on every desktop, and over an app that has taken the whole screen. None of that is a setting, and the hotkey is the control: ⌃⌥T sends the panel away from anywhere and brings it back the same way.

Driftwood never becomes the frontmost app, which is what makes the focus model work — and it is also why a panel below other windows would be a trap. Nothing would bring it forward again, and it could still be holding the keyboard behind whatever covered it.

Core

Tabs

Each tab is its own login shell, with its own scrollback and its own working directory. A background tab keeps running; it just draws nothing.

KeysDoes
⌘TNew tab
⌘WClose the current tab
⌘⇧] / ⌘⇧[ Next tab / previous tab. Both wrap around.
⌘1…⌘9 Jump to a tab by position
⌃⌥N Summon the panel and open a tab in it, from any app

The strip hides itself at one tab

The tab strip appears when there are two or more tabs and disappears when there is one, so a single-tab panel is nothing but terminal and you get that row of height back. It surprises people the first time; it is not a bug.

A tab's name

A tab shows whatever the shell last reported through the title escape sequence (OSC 0 or OSC 2) — which is what your prompt sets when it sets a window title. Failing that it shows the last path component of the working directory the shell reported, and failing that the name of the shell itself, so a stock zsh tab is called zsh.

A new tab starts where the old one is, if your shell says so

A new tab inherits the active tab's working directory only when the shell has reported it through OSC 7. A stock zsh with no prompt framework never sends that, so the fallback is your home directory. This is the common case, not an error.

To make zsh report it, add a prompt hook to ~/.zshrc:

# Report the working directory to the terminal on every prompt (OSC 7).
precmd() { printf '\e]7;file://%s%s\a' "$HOST" "$PWD" }

Prompt frameworks including Powerlevel10k, Starship and Oh My Zsh's default setup already do this, in which case there is nothing to add. Fish and recent bash setups on macOS emit it too.

Closing the last tab hides the panel

It does not quit Driftwood. The next ⌃⌥T brings the panel back with a fresh shell in it. To actually quit, use Quit in the right-click menu or ⌘Q.

Appearance

Themes & fonts

Four themes ship with the app and appear under Theme ▸ in the right-click menu. Each sets all sixteen ANSI colors plus five roles of its own: the panel background (with its own alpha), the default foreground, the cursor, the selection highlight and the tab strip's text.

The palettes are drawn from the app's own theme data, which needs JavaScript. The hex values are in TerminalTheme.swift.

Your own themes

Add a terminalThemes array to config.json and your themes join the built-ins in the Theme menu. Each entry needs an id, a title, and a colors object carrying all sixteen of ansi0 through ansi15. The five non-ANSI roles are optional and inherit from Driftwood Night when you leave them out. Values are #RRGGBB or #RRGGBBAA.

A theme missing even one ANSI color is rejected whole — it will not appear in the Theme menu at all, rather than loading with a gap in it. Turn on "debug": true to see which theme was rejected and why.

A complete example, Solarized Dark, which you can paste as it stands:

{
  "terminalThemes": [
    {
      "id": "solarized-dark",
      "title": "Solarized Dark",
      "colors": {
        "ansi0":  "#073642", "ansi1":  "#dc322f",
        "ansi2":  "#859900", "ansi3":  "#b58900",
        "ansi4":  "#268bd2", "ansi5":  "#d33682",
        "ansi6":  "#2aa198", "ansi7":  "#eee8d5",
        "ansi8":  "#002b36", "ansi9":  "#cb4b16",
        "ansi10": "#586e75", "ansi11": "#657b83",
        "ansi12": "#839496", "ansi13": "#6c71c4",
        "ansi14": "#93a1a1", "ansi15": "#fdf6e3",

        "background": "#002b36d9",
        "foreground": "#839496",
        "cursor":     "#93a1a1",
        "selection":  "#073642aa",
        "tabBarText": "#93a1a1"
      }
    }
  ]
}

The two hex digits on the end of background are its alpha: d9 is about 85% opaque, and the panel's blur shows through the rest. The Opacity menu multiplies that number rather than replacing it, so a theme that wants to be translucent stays relatively translucent at every opacity setting.

Changing one color: terminalPalette

Use terminalPalette to change individual colors on top of whichever theme is selected, without writing a whole theme. A value that does not parse costs you that one color and nothing else.

{
  "terminalPalette": {
    "ansi1": "#ff5f56",
    "cursor": "#72d6cf"
  }
}

The role names

The same names work in a custom theme's colors and in terminalPalette.

RoleIs
ansi0…ansi7 The normal colors, in order: black, red, green, yellow, blue, magenta, cyan, white.
ansi8…ansi15 The bright variants of the same eight, in the same order.
backgroundThe panel's tint, alpha included.
foregroundText with no color of its own.
cursorThe block cursor.
selectionThe highlight behind selected text. Give it an alpha. Selected text itself is drawn in foreground, so a selection is monochrome while it covers colored output.
tabBarTextTab titles. The strip's own fill is derived from background.

A palette decides what an ANSI color code renders as. It does not decide which code your prompt asks for. If your prompt is the wrong color in Driftwood, it is asking for a color number that your theme paints differently — the prompt's own configuration lives in your shell config and behaves the same way in every terminal.

Fonts

fontNames is a list of preferred fonts, best first. The first name that is actually installed wins; the rest are ignored. The default list is:

{
  "fontNames": [
    "MesloLGS NF",
    "MesloLGS Nerd Font",
    "Hack Nerd Font",
    "FiraCode Nerd Font",
    "JetBrainsMono Nerd Font",
    "Menlo"
  ]
}

The Nerd Fonts come first because prompt themes like Powerlevel10k draw their separators and icons from glyphs no stock macOS font carries. Install one of them if your prompt uses icons; without it, those characters render as empty boxes. Menlo is the last resort and ships with macOS, so the list always resolves to something.

A font name that is not installed is skipped silently. macOS returns nothing for an unknown font name rather than raising an error, so a typo costs you the font and says nothing about it. Set "debug": true and the log records which name actually resolved.

An empty list falls back to the defaults rather than leaving you with no font.

Font size and opacity

Both are fixed steps rather than sliders, and the current one is checkmarked in the menu. Font Size ▸ offers 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 and 16 point, and starts at 12. ⌘+ and ⌘− step through the same list and stop at each end rather than wrapping. Opacity ▸ offers 100%, 90%, 80%, 70% and 50%, each multiplied into the theme's own background alpha.

Automation

Quick commands

A quick command is a line of shell you have saved, reachable from the ⌃⌥K palette, from the Quick Commands ▸ submenu, and optionally from a global hotkey of its own.

{
  "quickCommands": [
    {
      "id": "logs",
      "title": "Tail logs",
      "command": "tail -f /tmp/app.log",
      "hotkey": "control+option+1"
    },
    {
      "id": "status",
      "title": "Git status",
      "command": "git status",
      "hotkey": "control+option+2",
      "run": true
    },
    { "id": "list", "title": "List files", "command": "ls" }
  ]
}
KeyRequiredNotes
idYes Any string, unique within the list. An entry with an empty or duplicate id is dropped.
titleNo What the palette and the menu show. Left out, the command text is used as the title.
commandYes The line to type. An entry with an empty command is dropped.
hotkeyNo Same grammar as the app's own bindings. Without one, the command is still in the palette and the menu.
runNo Whether to press Return after typing. Defaults to false. Read the next heading before setting it.

The run flag

run defaults to false, and that is a safety decision rather than a style one.

With "run": true, pressing that command's hotkey executes the command verbatim, in a real login shell, with your full environment, with no confirmation — and possibly while the panel is hidden, so the window that would show you what just happened is not even on screen.

Left at its default, the command is typed at the prompt and left sitting there for you to read and run yourself. Opting in is one word in config.json. Opting out after an rm -rf fired from a keystroke is not.

The panel is always summoned before a quick command fires, whichever way run is set, so a command never runs into a window you cannot see.

The palette

⌃⌥K opens the palette over the panel, and pressing it again closes it.

KeysDoes
Type Filters, case-insensitively, against both the title and the command text — so a command whose title you have forgotten is found by typing part of the command itself.
↑ / ↓Move the highlight
↩ Fire the highlighted command and close the palette
⎋Close it, changing nothing

Every row is labeled runs or types, so which of the two things Return is about to do is visible before you press it. You can try this on the front page: the hero re-creates the palette with the same three sample commands.

When an entry is wrong

A bad hotkey costs the hotkey; a bad command costs the entry. An entry whose hotkey does not parse, or collides with one of Driftwood's own bindings or with another quick command, keeps working from the palette and the menu with its hotkey ignored. Two bindings collide when they are the same keystroke, however you spelled them, so ctrl+alt+1 and control+option+1 are a collision. Every rejection is logged under "debug": true.

Settings

Configuration

Two files, both in ~/Library/Application Support/Driftwood/.

config.json — yours

Driftwood writes this file exactly once in its life, to create it with the defaults on first launch. There is no other write path, so nothing the app does can clobber a hand-edited setting. Changes take effect on the next launch; nothing re-reads the file while the app is running.

KeyTypeDefaultEffect
shellstring"/bin/zsh" The shell each tab launches, and the value exported to it as $SHELL. Both come from this one setting, so they cannot disagree. An empty string falls back to /bin/zsh.
shellArgumentsarray of string["-l"] Arguments passed to the shell. -l makes it a login shell, which is what sources the profile a normal terminal tab would.
fontNamesarray of string Five Nerd Fonts, then "Menlo" Preferred fonts, best first; the first installed name wins. An empty list falls back to the defaults.
hotkeysobjectSee Hotkeys The five global bindings.
terminalPaletteobjectabsent Per-role color overrides on top of the selected theme. An unparseable value costs that one role.
terminalThemesarray of objectabsent Your own themes, added to the Theme menu. Each needs all sixteen ANSI colors.
quickCommandsarray of objectabsent Saved commands for the palette, the menu and their own hotkeys.
debugbooleanfalse Writes a log to ~/Library/Logs/Driftwood/driftwood.log: the resolved font, hotkey registrations and failures, rejected themes and quick commands, and the focus state on every summon.

Every key is optional. A key you delete falls back to its default, and a key Driftwood does not recognize is ignored, so a config written for an older build never stops the app from starting, and an upgrade never asks you to migrate the file.

state.json — the app's

This one holds what the right-click menu changes, and Driftwood rewrites it several times in a session — dragging the panel alone rewrites it. Do not hand-edit it, since the app will overwrite what you write. Everything you set by hand lives in config.json, which Driftwood never touches.

KeyDefaultIs
frameabsent {x, y, width, height} in screen coordinates, written after a move or resize, and validated against the displays you actually have at the next launch.
theme"driftwood-night" The selected theme's id. An id that no longer exists falls back to the default rather than failing.
fontSize12 Snapped to the nearest preset when read.
opacity1.0 Clamped to 0.2–1.0, and multiplied into the theme's own background alpha.

A file that will not parse is moved aside, never overwritten

If either file becomes unreadable — a stray comma, a truncated write — Driftwood renames it to config.json.bak (or state.json.bak) and starts from the defaults. If a .bak is already there, the next one becomes .bak.1, then .bak.2, and so on, so an earlier rescue is never clobbered by a later one. Nothing you wrote is deleted; it is sitting next to the original path under a new name.

Shortcuts

Hotkey reference

Five global bindings, all editable in config.json, all registered system-wide so they work from whatever app you are in.

BindingDefaultDoes
toggle control+option+t Show the panel, give it the keyboard, or hide it if it already has the keyboard.
focus control+option+f Show the panel and take the keyboard, without a second press hiding it.
newTab control+option+n Show the panel and open a new tab in it.
commands control+option+k Open the quick-command palette, or close it if it is open.
quit "" — disabled Quit Driftwood, ending every shell in every tab. Off by default; set it only if you want that on a keystroke.

The syntax

A binding is modifiers and one key, joined by +, in any order. Case and spaces around the + are ignored, so "Control + Option + T" and "ctrl+alt+t" are the same binding.

PartAccepted spellings
Modifiers control or ctrl, option or alt, command or cmd, shift
Keys a–z, 0–9, f1–f12, space, tab, escape / esc, return / enter, delete / backspace

At least one of ⌃ ⌥ ⌘ is required

A binding must carry control, option or command. Shift does not count on its own, and a binding with no modifier at all is refused. Shift alongside another modifier is fine: "control+shift+k" is a valid binding.

A global hotkey takes that keystroke away from every other app for as long as Driftwood is running, which is why a bare key is not allowed — "space" would cost you the space bar everywhere.

Turning one off

Set it to "", "none" or "disabled". All three mean the same thing and none of them is logged as an error. A binding that is malformed rather than disabled is logged, so a typo is distinguishable from a decision.

{
  "hotkeys": {
    "toggle": "control+option+space",
    "focus": "none",
    "newTab": "control+option+n",
    "commands": "control+option+k",
    "quit": ""
  }
}

If a hotkey does nothing

macOS gives a global hotkey to whichever app registered it first, and tells the loser nothing. If another app already holds your binding, Driftwood's registration fails quietly. Change the binding in config.json, or quit the other app to find out which one it is. "debug": true logs every registration and every failure with its status code.

Troubleshooting

SymptomCause and fix
The hotkey does nothing Another app registered it first. macOS gives a global hotkey to whoever asked first and says nothing to the loser. Change the binding in config.json; "debug": true logs the failure.
I can't find the panel ⌃⌥T. If that does nothing, the row above is why. If the panel is somewhere off-screen, right-click it if you can reach any part of it and choose Reset Position; otherwise pkill -x Driftwood and relaunch, which re-validates the saved frame.
A theme I added doesn't appear It was rejected when the app read your config. A custom theme needs all sixteen of ansi0 through ansi15, and one missing color rejects the whole theme. "debug": true logs which theme and which reason.
My prompt's icons are boxes The font you asked for is not installed, and a missing name is skipped in silence. Install a Nerd Font, or set "debug": true and read which name actually resolved.
Pasted text is black until I type A known bug in the terminal component Driftwood uses, not fixable from here. Any redraw — typing a character, scrolling — restores the color.
The panel looks opaque whatever I set Almost always a custom theme whose background is a six-digit #RRGGBB, which means fully opaque. Opacity multiplies into the theme's own alpha rather than replacing it, so such a theme is a solid slab at 100% and only starts letting anything through at 90% and below. Give the color an eight-digit #RRGGBBAA value instead — the four built-ins sit between a6 and e0, and are translucent at every setting.
A completion script or prompt behaves differently than in Terminal.app Check echo $SHELL first; it names the shell in your shell setting. If that is right, check your profile is being read: a tab is a login shell only while shellArguments keeps -l.
A quick command's hotkey is ignored It failed to parse, or it collides with one of Driftwood's own bindings or with another quick command. The command itself still works from the palette and the menu. ctrl+alt+1 and control+option+1 are the same keystroke, so two entries spelled that way collide.

Almost every row above ends in the same place. Set "debug": true in config.json, relaunch, and read the log:

tail -f ~/Library/Logs/Driftwood/driftwood.log

Uninstall

If you installed with Homebrew, uninstall with Homebrew — deleting the app by hand leaves Homebrew still believing it is installed. brew uninstall --cask driftwood removes the app; brew zap --cask driftwood removes its settings and its log as well. Turn off Launch at Login before either, for the reason in step 1.

  1. Turn off Launch at Login first, if you ever turned it on. Right-click the panel and uncheck it. The login registration is held by macOS and outlives the app bundle, so deleting the app first leaves a registration behind that points at nothing.
  2. Quit Driftwood from the right-click menu, or run pkill -x Driftwood.
  3. Delete the app from Applications.
  4. Delete its settings and its log, if you want them gone:
rm -rf ~/Library/Application\ Support/Driftwood
rm -rf ~/Library/Logs/Driftwood

That is everything Driftwood writes. There are no preferences in defaults, no launch agent of its own, no receipts and nothing in /Library.
